## Introduction

This post lays out my personal thoughts on the vague fears or misconceptions someone might carry from "not having majored in CS" when they start learning to develop. The CS knowledge I'll bring up is something every developer's career should eventually pick up. What I want to get at here isn't that CS knowledge doesn't matter — it's about **when and how you approach learning it**.

At the stage where people are starting to study development, you can often see questions like "I didn't major in CS — what should I start with?" And plenty of education programs advertise themselves with titles like "a developer curriculum for people without a CS degree." Or, in some job postings, "a degree related to computer science" shows up as a listed qualification.

The sentence that draws a line between "majored" and "didn't major" carries the following assumptions built in:

- You can only become a developer if you know things that only CS majors would know.
- CS majors and non-majors will differ in how they take in the same development knowledge.

## Misconception 1: You have to know things only CS majors would know

First, we need to define the term. What does `things only a CS major would know` actually mean? I can't say for sure what whoever wrote that phrase intended, but my guess is it refers to knowledge from computer-science major coursework — things like operating systems, networking, data structures.

That's not to say the CS knowledge mentioned above doesn't matter to a developer. If anything, looking back on my own short career, it's important enough that I sometimes regret not paying closer attention in those classes.

### The knowledge you need depends on the moment

This can vary by field, but **you don't have to put every piece of knowledge taught in a computer-science curriculum to use just to start developing.** In the early stage, it can be more meaningful to focus on understanding what variables and functions mean, thinking hard about the code you're writing, and solving the various problems in front of you.

Knowledge that isn't used to solve the problems you're facing right now will gradually fade — its presence and content dimming, like an old item left in storage.

If, in the process of solving a problem, or through situations you run into directly or indirectly through your surroundings, you find you need a certain piece of knowledge, it's not too late to learn it then. Rather than stuffing everything into storage because you don't know what you'll need, the knowledge you pick up while resolving the situation right in front of you will stick with you far more vividly, and become truly "yours."

For example, if you're trying to improve performance and, while looking at a waterfall chart or a response header, you want to understand the terms you run into and go look up some networking knowledge — that's not too late to learn either, and I think what you learn in that moment has a higher chance of genuinely becoming your own.

### The knowledge you need also depends on the field

Development covers far too many fields to lump under one word, "development."

Take algorithms as an example: if the field or service you're aiming for is one that directly makes use of algorithms, that knowledge needs to become yours right away — but if not, I think it's fine to study other knowledge first. In other words, **whether you need algorithm knowledge when you're starting out** can differ from field to field.

When your goal is landing a job, that's a somewhat different conversation (since some companies do give algorithm tests), but I don't think you need to know algorithms before you can even start studying development.

> Of course, working through algorithm problems can help you get somewhat more comfortable with "writing code." But I think the "comfort" you gain from working on a variety of side projects is even greater than what you'd get from algorithm problems.

I used algorithms as an example, but the point isn't to study every piece of knowledge before picking a field — I think what matters is designing, for yourself, the roadmap you need for the direction you're aiming at.

## Misconception 2: CS majors and non-majors differ in how fast they understand things

I suspect this thinking is behind why so many technical courses are pitched "for non-majors." The idea seems to be that people without a CS background will struggle to grasp concepts like `variable` or `function`, and that every step of the flow needs to be explained "slowly."

In the end, that statement is neither wrong nor right. How fast someone understands something differs from person to person. Both majors and non-majors have a moment where they first encounter a concept they need in development, and how fast they understand it **differs person to person.**

Just because someone majored in CS doesn't mean they'll immediately understand an explanation full of difficult terminology, and just because someone didn't major in CS doesn't mean they can only understand things spelled out endlessly. It's a shame that something that can differ based on each person's individual experience or disposition gets split along a major/non-major frame instead.

## Misconception 3: Developers who came from a non-CS background are amazing(?)

You can find plenty of content on YouTube and blogs explaining "how someone **coming from** a non-CS background became a developer."

![native_dictionary](./images/native_dictionary.png)

The word "coming from," used so casually, carries a somewhat violent meaning at its root. Can "non-major" really be a kind of "origin" or "status"? The intent might be to capture the sense of being lost, or the experience, of starting a developer career from a field with nothing to do with development — but it's worth stepping back and asking whether this word, used without a second thought, is feeding into a frame of discrimination.

This isn't about belittling the effort of people who became developers without a CS background, or saying it isn't impressive. That effort is enormously impressive and valuable — but it doesn't mean people newly starting out as developers need to carry a vague fear because of this word.

**There's no such thing as something you can't do because you didn't major in CS, and no such thing as something you're automatically good at from the start because you did.**

## Wrapping Up

This post doesn't speak for everyone's situation — it's just a short reflection on a few situations I happened to see around me, a brief note about **fear**. If the vague fear you can carry when starting out turns into a sense of helplessness, that's no different from starting a race with a sandbag strapped to your ankle.
