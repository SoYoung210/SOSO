Retro season has rolled around again, right on schedule. I remember thinking last year too that 'time really flew by,' but this year feels even more that way.

I write this retrospective to look back, to record things, and so that someday I can look back at who I was around this time of year.

So how was my 2020?

- There's more I don't know than I used to.
- I ran [Banksalad Product Language](https://blog.banksalad.com/tech/banksalad-product-language-ios/) (hereafter BPL — the design system used at Banksalad), and it gave me a lot of experience, along with plenty to think about.
- I thought a lot about my role as a developer.
- I ran a conference as part of the FEConf organizing team.

I started my career in December 2018, so I'm now in my third year as a developer. A metric like 'years of experience' can only capture a small slice of what someone has actually gone through, but even so, each year that piles up feels a little intimidating. The number is visible, but the speed of growth isn't, so I think that's where the fear comes from — wondering whether my skills actually live up to being a '3rd-year developer.'

Looking back at my student days, I think I vaguely imagined that by the time I became a 3rd-year developer I'd be some kind of 'superhuman developer who could build anything, fast.' Now, instead of that kind of abstract goal, I want to think again each year about what kind of developer I actually want to become.

## There's More I Don't Know

Thinking back to this time last year, I was just starting out as a developer, and I remember feeling proud that my experience kept growing through all kinds of new experiences. I was learning the things in front of me that I didn't know, but back then I was mainly knocking down whatever problems were immediately in front of me, so I didn't have a good sense of 'what I don't know.'

Things I used to think were 'good enough' started to look different, and I realized that the standards I held in the past were built on a lot of ignorance.

Since I've studied and experienced a lot more than I had when I set 'those standards,' it makes sense in hindsight that they'd change. Still, back then I hadn't experienced standards actually changing, so I was a little too confident in my own opinions, and looking back, that's a bit embarrassing.

Next year, I want to study these keywords more:

- Good components and design
- Form Control
- Accessibility
- Animation
- Infra Structure
- GraphQL (w. Relay)

## BPL

I run Banksalad Product Language (hereafter BPL). Of all the projects I worked on this year, it's the one I learned the most from, the one I regret the most about, and the one I love the most.

The reason I say I regret it the most is because I want to do better. My teammates and I have been building out the design system through plenty of trial and error, and because of some decisions we weren't happy with early on, we're still migrating away from old rules. Doing it right from the very start would have been hard regardless, but looking back, there's a lot I wish had gone differently.

**'Worse than a bad decision is a slow decision, and worse than a slow decision is a decision that keeps getting reversed.'**

We managed to make all three kinds of decisions. I'll save the details for a separate post if I get the chance, but to put it briefly: for a long time, we overlooked the things that matter once something grows in scale.

It's hard to make a good decision every single time, but when making a decision, I want to think deeply while keeping in mind 'how important this package is to everyone using it' and 'what it looks like in the long run.' I'm grateful to have colleagues willing to think this hard about it alongside me.

Beyond the technical decisions, I got to personally feel just how important the small, careful details are when maintaining a library. Thinking about whether the teammate sitting next to me, or my team, would be happy with this tool required a completely different perspective from the development I usually do. Keywords like Breaking Release, backward compatibility, a contributor-friendly environment, and DX (Developer Experience) were things I thought hard about for the first time.

On the technical side, getting to experience the rules a package-level component has to follow, designing global components, and interactions let me broaden my range a little as a frontend developer.

## Thinking About My Role

Last year I mostly thought about how I could get better at my job. This year, I spent a lot of time thinking about what I should even be doing. It's less about individual tasks, and closer to asking what part of the work or role needed on my current team is something I can do well. Alongside new work, I also took time to reconsider work I'd already done.

**I want to play the role of "someone who suggests continuing what's good, and stopping what's bad without hesitation."** I want the idea written here — continuing what's good and stopping what's bad — to be something I share not just for myself, but with my teammates too. Next year, I'd like to have more time for tea time and mutual feedback with my team.

Code review is a good example of the good and bad from this year. During code review, I'd get caught up thinking about things from different angles, which sometimes slowed down the review back-and-forth, or made my reviews a bit too philosophical. Because I put so much effort into reviews in my own way, there were times it stung to hear 'that's hard given the schedule,' and those moments piled up into pressure.

Looking back now, that 'pressure' was an unnecessary monster I built myself, and the 'lots of reviews' was actually growth. If I try to pull out the Good and Bad from code review, it would look something like this.

- Good: Not hesitating to voice an opinion from a different angle. If that opinion came from my own ignorance, it becomes a chance to learn, and if not, it becomes a moment for all of us to think a bit more. I believe there's something to be gained from looking at a problem from a different angle.
- Bad: Feeling hurt 'because I put in the effort.' To borrow a line from [Leadership and Self-Deception](http://www.yes24.com/Product/Goods/11520753) (published in Korean as 'The Person Outside the Box'), I think I was inside the box. Even when doing the same thing, I want to stop doing it from inside the box (thinking of things centered on 'me').

## FEConf2020 at Home

I've been involved with FEConf since 2018, because I've received so much knowledge and help from the sharing the developer community practices, and I wanted to help that community grow and keep running in whatever way I could. I started as a volunteer, then as a speaker, and this year, as part of the organizing team.

While preparing [FEConf2020](https://2020.feconf.kr/), I had a great time working alongside people with such positive energy, and I got to feel firsthand how hard it is to run a community — which left me grateful, realizing that so many people were putting in far more effort for the development ecosystem than I'd imagined. I'm truly thankful to the fellow organizers who worked alongside me, the speakers who prepared great talks, and everyone who showed interest in the event.

Next year, I really hope the pandemic ends so we can hold the event in person again.

## Who Do I Want to Be in 2021?

I want to be a good colleague to the people around me. My idea of a 'good colleague' includes being someone trusted as a person who builds the service and contributes to making it better, someone who can directly solve teammates' problems with broad technical knowledge and experience, and someone who can share in each other's concerns.

I have several goals, but the one I want to write down here is studying Infra. I want to become a developer who understands and can personally look into everything involved in getting a web service I built deployed and delivered to users. I think I made a similar resolution last year, and I still feel like I've only gotten a rough sense of the overall flow.
